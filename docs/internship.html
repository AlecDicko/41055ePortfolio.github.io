Robotics Institute · Unpaid Internship

Internship Reflection

Written for my engineering ePortfolio, reflecting on expectation versus reality, the lessons that stuck, and what it changed about the kind of role I'm after.

What were your expectations about your internship before you joined?

I expected a conventional structure: work directly under my supervisor, take on discrete tasks and projects as they came up, and build experience mainly through that one-on-one relationship — essentially an extra pair of hands on my supervisor's existing workload, reactive rather than embedded in anything larger.

What was the reality? How was it different from your expectations?

That core expectation held — I do work closely with my supervisor on assigned tasks — but the role turned out broader than planned. I hadn't expected to take on a tutoring load, or to be slotted into other students' capstone projects to support their engineering work rather than just my own. Being personally invited to tutor by my thesis professor made clear early on that this wouldn't be a narrow task list; it positioned me as someone the department trusted to represent technical standards to other students, not just execute instructions. On balance, it's been a richer experience than the one I walked in expecting.

What lessons were the most important from your internship? Why were they important?

Communication and large-system organisation stand out. Supporting capstone students meant regularly recalibrating how I explain technical concepts — I noticed at a showcase that a level of detail which made sense to me lost my audience, and had to consciously adjust for who I was talking to. Communicating across the robotics institute more broadly showed me how much deliberate structure is needed to keep a large group of people moving in the same direction. It's a useful, humbling reminder of how much an institution's output depends on people simply staying on the same page.

After your workplace experience, what would you say your value proposition would be to an employer? How can you demonstrate this?

I'm a directed, hands-on worker comfortable integrating across electrical, electronic, mechanical and mechatronic systems rather than staying in one lane. That's demonstrated through iterative hardware work — building circuits and systems, then reconciling real bench measurements against theoretical predictions until they line up — and through a practical eye for automation and design developed while supporting other students' projects. Being trusted to teach hands-on skills like soldering to other students is itself evidence that my technical grounding, and my ability to communicate it, both hold up under scrutiny.

How did your internship influence the type of role in which you are interested?

It's clarified that I don't work well behind a desk. I'm most effective in a workshop, around physical systems and the day-to-day activity of a working lab. I'm confident machining when it's needed, but I've come away from this internship believing design and integration roles — shaping how systems fit together rather than just producing parts — suit me best.
